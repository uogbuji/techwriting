*On 26 July Israel Abiona asked me via LinkedIn to offer some thoughts for a blog post of his, "seniority in software engineering". I wrote this for him on the 28th*

In 2003 I had about a decade of experience, post engineering degree, in software development, and I'd been programming for some 18 years overall. That was a really tough time for many in the industry, thanks to the dot-com crash. I wrote an article for the Application Development Trend tech journal entitled ["Perspective on XML: A custom-fit career in app development"](https://adtmag.com/articles/2003/05/31/perspective-on-xml-a-customfit-career-in-app-development.aspx). This week, when I received a request to write about "seniority in software engineering", that article popped into my mind, and I re-read it, to find that so many of the points in it are still relevant to those seeking longevity in this industry.

In that article I suggested that rather than follow the obvious trends of the most popular languages, techniques, platforms, etc., developers should remain endlessly curious, and always be ready to embrace new or less heralded technologies which really speak to their personal style and interests. They should also build social skills and learn how to collaborate in frontier spaces, as well as how to sell their characteristic journeys and preferences.

That article itself was part of a column I was writing for that journal, and that meta-fact is also relevant. I have always enjoyed writing, and early in my career I thought it would be cool to write for the sorts of journals I always read while learning and discovering about development. I made a few cold submissions, and formed relationships with whichever editors were favorable. I had to learn, often the hard way, from such editors how writing for tech journal audiences was very different from the poetry and prose that excited my literary sensibilities. In time I became a very prolific columnist, and that even supplemented my income in times when consulting opportunities were slow to emerge. Writing brought me to the attention of conference organizers looking for speakers, opening up another way for me to build a portfolio.

The point is not that every developer should also be a writer, but that every developer has some extra something to their individuality that they should find ways to embrace in complement to textbook software matters. They should also exploit any and all social openings that their unique background surfaces. Software development is a social activity, and it is also a multi-disciplinary activity, and I think embracing both of these facts is key to warding against burnout in the short term, and enjoying a long, rewarding career in the long run.
